Maths · Topic 01

Financial Mathematics: Simple Interest & Commission

Why this matters

Whenever someone saves money at a bank, borrows from a lender, or sells goods for a company on a percentage basis, they are using simple interest or commission. Both are just percentages applied to money — the trick is knowing which formula and which numbers to plug in.

Simple interest, from first principles

Simple interest is a fixed reward (or charge) calculated only on the original amount, called the Principal (P), every year, at a fixed Rate (R), for a number of Time periods (T), usually years. Unlike compound interest, it never grows the "base" — year two earns exactly the same interest as year one.

I = (P × R × T) ÷ 100

Once you know the interest (I), the total amount (A) owed or received is simply the principal plus the interest:

A = P + I

Example. Tendai deposits $400 into a savings account paying 6% simple interest per year. How much interest has he earned after 3 years, and what is the account worth?

P = 400, R = 6, T = 3

I = (400 × 6 × 3) ÷ 100 = 7200 ÷ 100 = $72

A = 400 + 72 = $472

Common exam trap If time is given in months, convert it to years first by dividing by 12 (e.g. 8 months = 8/12 years) before substituting into the formula.

Commission

Commission is money paid to a salesperson or agent as a percentage of the value of what they sold. It rewards performance rather than time, so there is no "T" in the formula at all:

Commission = (Rate ÷ 100) × Sales Value

Example. A real-estate agent earns 2.5% commission on every house she sells. She sells a house for $85,000. What is her commission?

Commission = (2.5 ÷ 100) × 85,000

Commission = 0.025 × 85,000 = $2,125

Basic salary plus commission Many jobs pay a fixed basic salary plus commission on sales above a threshold. Always calculate the two parts separately, then add them: Total pay = Basic salary + Commission earned.

Maths · Topic 02

Decimals, Fractions & Percentages

Three faces of the same number

A decimal, a fraction, and a percentage are three different ways of writing the exact same value. Being able to move freely between them is what makes calculations like "30% off" or "share this in a ratio of 3:5" quick instead of confusing.

Real-life calculation 1 — market discount

Example. A pair of shoes costs $18. The shop offers 15% off. What is the new price?

Discount = 15% of 18 = (15 ÷ 100) × 18 = 0.15 × 18 = $2.70

New price = 18 − 2.70 = $15.30

Real-life calculation 2 — sharing a harvest by fraction

Example. A family harvests 240 kg of maize. They keep 3/8 of it for the household and sell the rest. How many kg do they sell?

Kept = 3/8 × 240 = (3 × 240) ÷ 8 = 720 ÷ 8 = 90 kg

Sold = 240 − 90 = 150 kg

Real-life calculation 3 — recipe scaling with decimals

Example. A recipe for 4 people needs 0.75 kg of flour. How much flour is needed for 10 people?

Flour per person = 0.75 ÷ 4 = 0.1875 kg

For 10 people = 0.1875 × 10 = 1.875 kg

Reading the exam question "Of" almost always means multiply ("15% of 18"), "per" means divide ("kg per person"), and "out of" describes a fraction ("90 out of 240").

Physics · Topic 01

Electricity Installation in Rural Areas: Assembling a Solar-Powered Grid

The problem

Many rural homesteads and schools are far from the national electricity grid, but the sun reaches every roof for free. A small standalone ("off-grid") solar system converts sunlight directly into usable electricity for lighting, phone charging, and radios, without needing a connection to Zesa lines.

The four components you are assembling

  • Solar panel — converts sunlight into DC electricity via the photovoltaic effect.
  • Charge controller — regulates the current flowing from the panel into the battery so it doesn't overcharge or get damaged.
  • Battery — stores electrical energy as chemical energy, so there is still power after sunset.
  • Inverter (optional) / DC loads — either converts stored DC into AC for normal appliances, or powers DC loads (LED bulbs, small radios) directly.

Assembling the system

  1. Mount the solar panel facing north (in the Southern Hemisphere) at a tilt angle close to your local latitude, with no shade falling on it during the day.
  2. Run cables from the panel's positive and negative terminals to the "solar in" terminals of the charge controller — never straight to the battery.
  3. Connect the battery to the charge controller's "battery" terminals, matching positive to positive and negative to negative.
  4. Connect your load (LED lights, a DC radio, or an inverter for AC appliances) to the controller's "load" output.
  5. Switch on and confirm the controller's indicator shows charging during the day and safe discharge at night.

The physics behind sizing the system

The two formulas below let you check whether a panel and battery are actually big enough for what you want to run — this is the calculation examiners expect you to do, and the one that stops a real installation from failing on the first cloudy day.

Power (P, in watts) = Voltage (V) × Current (I)
Energy stored (Wh) = Battery Voltage (V) × Battery Capacity (Ah)

Example. A homestead runs three 10 W LED bulbs for 5 hours each night. How much energy (in watt-hours) is needed per night, and what size 12 V battery (in Ah) should store it?

Total power = 3 × 10 W = 30 W

Energy needed = Power × Time = 30 W × 5 h = 150 Wh

Required battery capacity = Energy ÷ Voltage = 150 ÷ 12 = 12.5 Ah (choose the next standard size up, e.g. 20 Ah, to allow for cloudy days and battery losses)

Why installers oversize the battery Batteries should not be drained below about 50% of their rated capacity, or they wear out fast. That is why real installations double the calculated Ah value as a safety margin.

Physics · Topic 02 — HBC project

Turning Cattle Dung Into Cooking Energy: A Simple Biogas Digester

The problem

Most rural households cook with firewood, which takes hours of walking to collect and adds smoke to the home. Almost every rural homestead in Zimbabwe already keeps cattle — and cattle dung, when it rots without air, releases a flammable gas (mostly methane) that can be piped straight to a stove. This is a genuine energy-transformation project: chemical energy in waste becomes heat energy for cooking, using nothing but airtight containers and physics.

The physics idea: anaerobic digestion

When dung and water are sealed away from oxygen (an anaerobic, or airless, environment), bacteria break down the organic matter and release biogas as a by-product. Because the container is sealed, the gas produced has nowhere to go except to build up pressure — and that pressure is exactly what pushes the gas along a pipe to the stove, the same way pressure differences drive gas through any pipe.

Building a basic household digester

  1. Mix fresh cattle dung with water in roughly a 1:1 ratio in a drum until it reaches the thickness of a thin porridge, called the "slurry."
  2. Pour the slurry into a sealed digester tank (an old drum or brick chamber works) fitted with an inlet pipe and a gas outlet at the top.
  3. Seal every joint airtight — any leak lets oxygen in, which stops the anaerobic bacteria from working properly.
  4. Fit a flexible storage bag or an inverted floating drum over the gas outlet; as gas is produced, it collects here and its own weight keeps the system under gentle, steady pressure.
  5. Run a pipe from the gas store to a modified stove burner; open the valve and light the burner as you would with any gas cooker.
  6. Remove the spent slurry from an overflow outlet every few weeks and use it as a nutrient-rich fertiliser for crops — nothing is wasted.

The physics behind estimating gas output

A commonly used field estimate is that one kilogram of fresh cattle dung yields approximately 0.03 to 0.04 cubic metres of biogas. This lets you estimate, from the size of a herd, roughly how much gas a household can expect per day — the same kind of proportional calculation used for the solar sizing above.

Gas yield (m³) = Mass of dung (kg) × Yield rate (m³ per kg)

Example. A homestead collects 25 kg of fresh dung per day, and the digester yields 0.035 m³ of biogas per kg of dung. How much biogas is produced daily, and roughly how many hours of cooking (at 0.2 m³ per hour of use) does that provide?

Gas yield = 25 × 0.035 = 0.875 m³ per day

Cooking time = 0.875 ÷ 0.2 = about 4.4 hours of stove use per day
